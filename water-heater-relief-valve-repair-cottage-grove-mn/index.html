Water Heater Relief Valve Repair in Cottage Grove, MN

A temperature and pressure relief valve, usually called the T&P valve, is the safety device that keeps a water heater from becoming a pressure vessel. If yours is dripping, hissing or leaking onto the floor, the valve is telling you something, and it deserves attention the same day.

This guide explains what the valve does, why it drips, how to test it safely and when a repair is more than a weekend job. It is written for Cottage Grove homeowners, with notes on local rules and a short checklist for what to do first.

What a relief valve does

The T&P valve sits on the side or top of the tank and opens if water pressure or temperature gets too high. Most are rated to open at 150 psi or at 210 degrees Fahrenheit. When it opens, water flows through a discharge pipe to a safe place, relieving the pressure before the tank can fail.

It is a mechanical part with a spring and a seat, and like any such part it wears. Hard water minerals can foul the seat, and a valve that has not been tested in years may stick closed when you need it most.

Why it drips

CauseWhat it looks likeWhat to do
Thermal expansion in a closed systemDrips after the heater runs, stops when the water coolsMeasure pressure, add an expansion tank
High supply pressureDrips constantly, pressure above normal on a gaugeAdd or adjust a pressure reducing valve
Worn or fouled valve seatSlow drip with normal pressureReplace the valve
Overheated waterHissing or steam at the valveShut off the heater and call right away
Failed thermostatWater too hot at the tapReplace the control or thermostat

A drip does not always mean the valve is bad. The most common cause in a modern home is thermal expansion. When a backflow preventer or pressure regulator closes the supply, heated water has nowhere to go, and the extra volume pushes the valve open. An expansion tank gives the water room to expand.

When to stop and call right away

If the valve is steaming, spraying or the water at your taps is scalding, turn the heater to its lowest setting or off at the breaker or gas control and call. A tank that is overheating is dangerous.

The same is true if the relief valve has no discharge pipe, or the pipe is capped or plugged. Never cap a relief valve.

How to test the valve safely

  1. Place a bucket under the discharge pipe, since hot water will come out
  2. Lift the test lever gently and let it flow for a few seconds
  3. Release it and confirm it snaps closed with no drip
  4. If it keeps dripping or the lever is stuck, plan to replace it
  5. Never hold the lever open long, because the water can scald

Manufacturers often recommend testing once a year. A valve that does not flow when you lift the lever, or does not close fully afterward, is a candidate for replacement.

How a valve is replaced

1

Shut down the heater

Turn off the gas or power and close the cold water supply valve.

2

Relieve pressure and drain

Open a hot tap, then drain several gallons from the tank so the water level drops below the valve.

3

Swap the valve

Unscrew the old valve, apply thread sealant to the new one and tighten it into the tank opening.

4

Reconnect and refill

Attach the discharge pipe, refill the tank, run a hot tap to purge air and check for leaks.

The new valve must have the same rating and be sized for the heater. The discharge pipe should run downward and end where water is visible and safe. Local code governs details, so follow the installation manual and ask your building department.

If the valve is old, rusted in place or the tank has leaks, the safer step is a water heater repair visit. If the tank is near the end of its life, a replacement may cost less than repeated repairs.

When the real problem is pressure

Incoming water pressure that is too high stresses valves, fixtures and the tank. A simple gauge on a hose bib shows your pressure. Many homes aim for a range that is firm without being punishing, and a pressure reducing valve brings it down when the supply is too strong.

If the pressure is normal and the valve still drips, test the expansion tank. A waterlogged tank loses its air cushion and stops absorbing expansion. A plumber can check and recharge or replace it.

Replace the heater or fix the valve?

Fix the valve

  • The tank is not leaking
  • The heater is not old
  • The pressure checks out
  • The cost is small

Replace the heater

  • The tank shell is leaking
  • The unit is near its expected life
  • Repeated repairs add up
  • Sediment and rust are heavy

Cottage Grove note: code, permits and who to ask

Water heater work in Minnesota falls under the Minnesota Plumbing Code, and the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry is the state agency that sets the rules. Where a city is the administrative authority, its inspectors handle plumbing inspections. Follow the manufacturer's guidelines for the valve rating shown on the tank label.

In practice that means a replacement valve is a minor job, while moving or replacing the heater may need a permit. A short checklist

  • Look at the discharge pipe, which should be present and open
  • Test the lever once a year
  • Note whether the drip comes with a heating cycle
  • Check your water pressure with a gauge
  • Call a plumber if you see steam, scalding water or a stuck lever
