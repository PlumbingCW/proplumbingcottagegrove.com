What a good installation covers

A water heater is more than the tank. The installation includes shutoff valves, a temperature and pressure relief valve with a proper discharge pipe, flexible or hard-piped connections, a drain pan where needed, correct venting for gas units and an electrical or gas connection that meets code.

Shortcuts show up later as leaks, backdrafting or a relief valve that drips. Doing each of those parts right the first time is what makes the heater last.

Parts of a proper install

  • Shutoff valve on the cold supply
  • Temperature and pressure relief valve with a full discharge pipe
  • Drain pan with a drain line when the heater sits above finished space
  • Dielectric or approved fittings where dissimilar metals meet
  • Expansion tank when the supply has a closed system
  • Secured venting with correct slope and clearances on gas units

Missing a single item on this list is the usual reason an install fails an inspection.

Location and space

In most east metro homes the heater lives in the basement or a utility room. That is good news, since a leak can be contained by a drain pan and a floor drain. In a finished basement or an upstairs closet, a drain pan and a discharge path are essential.

If you are moving a heater to a new location, the plan should include new venting, a new gas or electrical connection and clearance for service. Those steps are why a planned installation costs differently from a straight swap.

Heat pump water heaters in a cold climate

Heat pump water heaters draw heat from the surrounding air, so they work best in a space that stays above freezing and has room to breathe. A basement utility room next to the furnace is often a good fit, though they can cool the space slightly and produce condensate that needs a drain.

They use far less electricity than a standard electric tank. They also cost more up front and may qualify for rebates, so check what is available for your utility and your household before you decide.

Questions to settle before scheduling

  • Which fuel and size best fit your household
  • Where the heater will sit and whether the space allows it
  • Whether venting, a gas line or an electrical circuit needs work
  • Whether a permit applies in your city
  • Whether an expansion tank or a mixing valve is a good idea

Gas, electric and combustion air

Gas heaters need a supply of fresh air for combustion and a vent that carries exhaust outside. A heater in a tight utility room or a finished closet may need louvers or a direct vent design. Electric units need the right breaker and wire size.

These requirements are the reason a unit that fit the old heater does not always fit the new one. Checking them first prevents surprises on install day and keeps the heater safe and efficient.

Code, permits and standards

A new installation must meet the Minnesota Plumbing Code, and the manufacturer's guidelines set clearances, venting and electrical needs. Cities often act as the administrative authority, so ask the City of Cottage Grove building department what it requires.
