Install a Garbage Disposal on a Single Sink, Cottage Grove MN

A garbage disposal on a single-bowl sink is a straightforward upgrade, but it has more steps than most people expect. You are mounting a heavy unit under the sink, connecting it to the drain and to power, and in many kitchens linking the dishwasher too.

This guide walks through the installation, explains the parts that trip people up and shows when it is smarter to call a plumber.

What you need to start

  • A disposal sized for your household
  • A disposal mounting kit, if not included
  • Plumber's putty
  • A new discharge elbow and gasket
  • A power cord kit if the unit does not have one
  • Screwdrivers, pliers, a hex key and a bucket

Check the cabinet first. A single sink with a shallow cabinet or a tall trap may limit which unit fits. Measure the clearance from the sink bottom to the cabinet floor, and compare it with the disposal's height.

Single bowl versus double bowl

A single-bowl sink has one drain opening, so the disposal connects directly to the drain and sends everything to one trap. A double sink usually has a continuous waste pipe linking two drains. The single-bowl layout is simpler, and it makes it easy to fit the disposal in line with the trap.

The detail to watch is the trap arm height. The disposal outlet sits at a fixed height, and the trap must connect at or below it so wastewater drains by gravity. You may need to adjust the trap or the trap arm.

Installation steps

1

Remove the old parts

Unplug or switch off power, disconnect the trap and remove the old strainer or disposal.

2

Install the sink flange

Roll a rope of putty around the flange, press it into the drain opening and tighten the mounting assembly from below.

3

Hang the disposal

Lift the unit onto the mounting ring, rotate it until it locks and tighten the mounting screws.

4

Connect the drain and power

Attach the discharge elbow and trap, connect the dishwasher hose if present and wire or plug the unit in.

The dishwasher knockout

If a dishwasher drains into the disposal, there is a small plastic plug inside the dishwasher inlet called a knockout. It must be removed before use, or the dishwasher will not drain. Fish out the plug after knocking it out with a screwdriver and hammer, since a stray plug can jam the grinder.

Many installers forget this step. The sign is a dishwasher that leaves water in the bottom after a cycle. If that happens after an install, the knockout is the first thing to check.

Power: cord or hardwire

Disposals either plug into an outlet under the sink or are hardwired to a switch. A cord kit makes a plug connection possible. A hardwired unit needs correct wiring and a grounded connection.

Electrical work around water has risks. If you have no existing outlet or switch, or you are unsure, ask a licensed electrician or a plumber with electrical credentials. Turn off the breaker before touching any wires.

Test the install

  • Run water and turn on the disposal for a few seconds
  • Check every fitting for drips with a dry towel
  • Run the dishwasher and watch the drain connection
  • Listen for unusual noise or vibration
  • Check again the next day

A slow drip at the flange or the trap is much easier to fix now than after a month.

When to hire a plumber

DIY is a fit when

  • You are replacing an existing disposal
  • The sink and cabinet fit the new unit
  • You are comfortable with basic tools and wiring

Call a plumber when

  • You are adding a disposal for the first time
  • The drain or trap needs changes
  • There is no outlet or switch
  • The kitchen drain is slow or old

Noise, size and horsepower

Disposals range from small units that handle light scraps to larger ones with more grinding power and extra insulation. A bigger motor jams less and runs more quietly because it does not strain. For most households, a mid-range unit is a good value, and a bigger one is worth it for heavy cooking.

Look at the feed type too. A continuous feed unit turns on with a switch and runs while you feed it. A batch feed unit runs only when a stopper is in place, which is safer around children but slower to use.

A disposal swap in the same spot is a repair. Wiring and a new drain connection are different, because the Minnesota Plumbing Code and electrical rules start to apply, and so can a permit.
