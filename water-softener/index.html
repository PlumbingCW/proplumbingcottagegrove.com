Test before you buy

Water hardness and quality vary by water system, and the right solution depends on what is actually in your water. Ask City of Cottage Grove Public Works for the annual water quality report, or use a test kit or a lab test on your tap. A plumber can also take a field reading.

We do not guess at your hardness. If your water is not hard, a softener is a waste of money, and if you have a different concern such as taste or odor, a filter may be the better tool.

How a softener works

A softener holds resin beads that trap hardness minerals and release sodium or potassium in exchange. When the beads are full, the unit regenerates with salt brine, flushes and starts again. It needs salt, a drain connection and an electrical outlet.

Installing it correctly means the bypass, the drain, the brine line and the placement ahead of the water heater. A poorly installed unit wastes salt, and one connected to the wrong lines can soften cold water to outdoor spigots that do not need it.

Where a softener goes in the plumbing

A whole-house softener is installed on the main supply after the water meter and before the water heater, so every hot and cold line is protected. Outdoor spigots are usually left on hard water, since lawn watering does not need softened water and it wastes salt.

The unit needs space for a brine tank, a drain nearby for regeneration and a bypass valve so you can service it without shutting off the whole house. In older Cottage Grove basements, finding a clean drain path is often the biggest design question, and a plumber can solve it with a standpipe or a small pump.

Code, permits and standards

Softener installation follows the Minnesota Plumbing Code, including the air gap and drain connection. Treatment claims depend on the product being certified for the specific contaminant, and the Minnesota Department of Health publishes guidance on drinking water. According to your water utility's annual report, you can decide whether treatment is worth adding.
