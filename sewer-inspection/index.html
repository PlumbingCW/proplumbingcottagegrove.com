What a sewer camera shows

A small waterproof camera on a flexible cable travels down the line from a clean-out, sending live video to a monitor. The plumber watches for what a cable cannot detect: cracks, offset joints, roots at connections, scale, bellies where water sits and the point where the line meets the city main.

Many cameras also include a locating transmitter, so the depth and position of a defect can be marked on the ground. That information shapes whether a fix is a small spot repair or a bigger project.

What the camera findsWhat it tells youLikely next step
Roots at a jointWater is entering at a connectionClean the line and plan a joint repair
Standing water in a low spotThe pipe has a bellyMonitor, or regrade that section
Cracked or fractured pipeThe wall is failingSpot repair, lining or replacement
Heavy scale or greaseThe pipe wall is narrowedHydro jetting
Clean pipe, no defectsThe problem is elsewhereCheck venting or the branch line

When to schedule one

Good reasons to book a camera inspection

  • You are buying a home, especially one built before the 1990s
  • A drain has backed up more than once in a year
  • The main line was cabled and clogged again
  • Large trees stand over the sewer path
  • You are planning work that digs near the line
  • You see wet soil or a sunken patch in the yard

A camera is a diagnostic, not a repair. Most visits end with a clear answer and no digging.

Buying or selling a home in the east metro

A standard home inspection does not run a camera down the sewer. If you are buying in Cottage Grove, Hastings or nearby towns, especially a home on an older lot, a camera check can reveal a cracked line before closing. That is a negotiating tool, and it is far cheaper than finding the problem after you move in.

Sellers use it too. A clean video report removes a source of doubt for a buyer, and a known issue can be priced into the sale rather than surprising everyone at the last minute.

Reading the video with you

After the run you can look at the monitor with the plumber, and we describe what each finding means and what it does not. A root hair at a joint is a maintenance issue. A fractured pipe is a repair. A belly may be fine for years or may be the reason the line clogs.

You should leave knowing whether you need a cleaning, a repair or nothing at all. If you need a repair, sewer line repair covers the options, and we confirm the price before work starts.

What the camera cannot do

A camera sees the pipe, not the soil around it, and it cannot see past a full blockage. If a line is badly clogged, it has to be cleared before the camera can pass, which is why a cleaning visit sometimes comes first.

It also does not replace a locate. Before any digging, call 811. A plumber coordinates it, but the call is required.

  • Plan for about an hour on site for a typical residential line
  • Make sure the clean-out is accessible
  • Tell us about past backups and repairs
  • Ask for a copy of the findings if you need them for a sale or an insurer

What a report should include

  • The location of the clean-out and the length of pipe inspected
  • The pipe material and any change in diameter
  • Each defect, such as roots, cracks or offsets, with its approximate distance
  • Whether water stands in a low spot
  • A plain recommendation, such as monitor, clean or repair

A good report is something you can hand to a buyer, a seller, an insurer or another contractor. If a repair is needed, it gives each party the same facts, and it keeps the quote tied to a defect rather than a guess.

Code, permits and standards

A camera inspection is a diagnostic, so no permit is involved. The findings can trigger code questions later. If a repair is needed, the Minnesota Plumbing Code applies, and guidelines from the pipe manufacturer decide whether lining is an option. Ask for a written note of what the camera found, so a buyer, seller or inspector can rely on the same facts.
